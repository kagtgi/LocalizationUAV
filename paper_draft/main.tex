\begin{abstract}
Accurate geo-localization of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in GPS-denied
environments remains a core challenge for autonomous navigation.
We study a scene-agnostic, training-free approach that seeks the UAV's 2-D
geographic position by matching purely geometric building-footprint descriptors
extracted from a single nadir image against a geo-referenced satellite map, and
we provide a rigorous analysis of where and why this appealing paradigm
succeeds and fails.
Buildings are segmented by a pre-trained Mask R-CNN; each polygon is
decomposed into triangles by Constrained Delaunay Triangulation (CDT), and
each triangle is encoded with a 5-dimensional descriptor
$f = (\alpha_1,\alpha_2,e_1,e_2,e_3)$ comprising two sorted interior angles
and three \textbf{Maximal Free Cone Angles (MFCA)}---a geometric quantity
measuring the largest obstacle-free angular sector at each vertex.
The MFCA is directly inspired by Ekeland's variational principle (1974):
the principle guarantees a critical point where no improvement is achievable
without a proportional distance cost; we instantiate this as a
\emph{kernel expansion} $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$---the largest polygon formed by
merging adjacent CDT triangles such that all three original triangle vertices
remain on the boundary of the result.
This stopping criterion is the direct geometric analogue of Ekeland's
cone-criticality condition: the polygon cannot be expanded further while
keeping the triangle vertices as boundary (critical) points.
The Ekeland angle $e_v$ at each vertex is then the exterior angle of
$P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ at $v$, computed in $O(1)$ per vertex by an
edge-anchored half-plane construction with no sweep required.
Satellite patch descriptors are indexed offline in a KD-tree ($\ell_1$ metric);
online matching runs in $O(M\log N)$ time per query.
We evaluate this pipeline rigorously on the UAV-VisLoc benchmark and report a
central negative result with a precise diagnosis.
Building coverage at the true location is high ($93\%$), yet single-frame
descriptor retrieval fails at city scale: the geometrically correct match is
essentially never among the top-ranked candidates (retrieval ${<}1\%$) and
localization error remains at the kilometre level.
This ceiling is one of descriptor \emph{discriminability}, not of coverage or
feature scaling---enriching the descriptor with building size and a log-polar
building-constellation context, matching at building rather than triangle
granularity, and normalizing feature dimensions each shrink the true-match
descriptor distance by up to $16\times$ yet leave retrieval near zero.
Following the coarse-to-fine, particle-filter paradigm of successful vector-map
methods, we further integrate the per-frame matches over a flight trajectory
with a Gaussian-mixture observation and a particle filter; this too fails to
converge, because the true position receives recurring observation support in
only $12\%$ of frames.
This limit is terrain-general: across five UAV-VisLoc sites spanning urban,
coastal, hilly, built-up, and arid terrain, meter-level accuracy stays below
$3\%$ within $500$\,m with kilometre-scale median error.
We conclude that the limiting factor is the \emph{observation model}: purely
geometric per-element matching does not supply the recurring, spatially
consistent support that sequential estimation requires at scale, and must be
paired with a stronger area-level observation.
Acting on this diagnosis, we show the fix is constructive and keeps the Ekeland
angle central: aggregating the descriptor over ${\approx}200$\,m cells---as a
histogram of per-building Ekeland free-cone angles alongside building density
and layout---escapes the per-element ceiling. Against real re-implemented
baselines (BRM, CFBVM's concentric-ring descriptor) under identical protocol,
this pooled, statistically significant improvement in coarse-localization
recall ($+2.9$--$4.4$ percentage points, $p{\le}0.02$, $n{=}272$ frames across
five sites) is not universal per site---the Ekeland angle is the best
performer on three of five terrains. The Ekeland free-cone angle is thus a
complementary, discriminative shape cue at the area level on most, not all,
terrains, though absolute accuracy remains coarse.
Our analysis provides the missing empirical account of why training-free
geometric matching, though attractive, does not by itself scale---and where the
Ekeland descriptor nonetheless adds measurable value.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

UAVs depend on GNSS for position fixes in nearly every operational domain:
search-and-rescue, precision agriculture, infrastructure inspection, and
military reconnaissance.
Yet GNSS is unavailable in urban canyons, jammed in contested airspace, and
unreliable indoors---scenarios that are precisely those requiring the most
reliable autonomy.
A single GNSS outage during a search-and-rescue operation can redirect a UAV
kilometres from the target zone.
In contested airspace, GPS jamming has been documented to deny localization
entirely for extended periods~\cite{murrian2021gnss,dovis2015gnss}.
For emergency responders, infrastructure inspectors, and autonomous delivery
operators, GNSS failure is not a theoretical edge case---it is the operational
norm in precisely the environments these platforms are deployed.
Vision-based absolute visual localization (AVL) addresses this gap by
recovering the UAV's 2-D geographic position from onboard camera images matched
against a pre-stored reference map, with no satellite signal
required~\cite{couturier2021review,ouyang2024cfbvm}.

The dominant difficulty in UAV-to-satellite AVL is the \emph{cross-view
appearance gap}: satellite maps are orthorectified nadir projections captured
in a fixed season, while UAV images arrive at varying altitude, heading, roll,
pitch, time of day, and season.
Existing methods expose a fundamental trilemma:
(i)~\emph{Appearance-based methods} (SIFT, ORB, deep cross-view
networks~\cite{goforth2019gps,fan2024sspt}) rely on pixel-level signals that
change across season, altitude, and lighting---SIFT achieves zero Recall@10 on
MAFS-10 after 1,630\,s of computation~\cite{yuan2025swapf},
confirming that texture descriptors cannot bridge the nadir gap.
(ii)~\emph{Deep learning methods} overcome the appearance gap by learning
invariant embeddings, but require scene-specific retraining, making them
impossible to deploy on a new city map within hours.
(iii)~\emph{Vector map methods} exploit shape rather than texture, but existing
approaches (BRM~\cite{choi2020brm}, CFBVM~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm}) operate on
whole-building statistics and lose the \emph{spatial context between buildings},
making them poorly discriminative in dense urban blocks where many buildings
share similar individual shapes.
No existing training-free method simultaneously encodes both triangle-level
shape and the structural context of the surrounding building footprint within
a single descriptor.

Our key insight is that \emph{building footprint geometry} is stable across
all of these appearance variations: a polygon extracted from a nadir UAV image
and the same polygon in a satellite map agree in shape regardless of altitude,
season, or illumination.
We exploit this by (i)~decomposing each building polygon into triangles via CDT
and (ii)~encoding each triangle with a 5-D descriptor of pure angular
quantities invariant to scale, rotation, and appearance.
The free-space component of this descriptor---the Maximal Free Cone Angle
(MFCA)---is directly inspired by Ekeland's variational principle~\cite{ekeland1974}:
the principle characterises a point of maximal local stability as the apex
of the largest cone that touches but does not cross a function's graph;
we instantiate this cone not over a function graph but over a 2-D polygon
boundary, yielding a scalar that encodes \emph{how much local geometric
freedom exists at each building vertex}.

Training-free geometric localization is an appealing route to
\emph{map-portable autonomy}---a platform pre-loaded with satellite imagery of
any new city operating immediately, with no training phase.
This paper asks, and answers rigorously, whether purely geometric per-element
matching can deliver it: we find that it cannot at city scale, and we
characterize precisely why, turning an aspirational claim into a measured
account of the paradigm's limits and of what a viable observation model must
provide.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Edge-anchored Ekeland angle (MFCA).}
      We derive the Ekeland angle $e_v$ from first principles via a two-phase
      construction: (i)~expand the kernel triangle into a maximal polygon
      $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ whose stopping condition is geometrically analogous
      to Ekeland's cone-criticality---merging halts precisely when any of the
      three original triangle vertices would leave the boundary; (ii)~compute
      $e_v$ as the exterior angle of $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ at $v$ via an
      edge-anchored half-plane selection (Section~\ref{sec:mfca}).
      The result is \emph{exact} (no quantization), $O(1)$ per vertex, and
      geometrically motivated: the kept sector's bounding ray is tangent to the
      polygon boundary, the same picture as Ekeland's ``cone touches but does
      not cross'' condition.
\item \textbf{5-D geometric feature and matching pipeline.}
      A descriptor $f = (\alpha_1,\alpha_2,e_1,e_2,e_3)$ combining two sorted
      interior angles and three sorted MFCAs (Section~\ref{sec:feat}), with a
      complete training-free pipeline---instance segmentation, CDT, KD-tree
      index ($\ell_1$, $O(M\log N)$ online matching), and a
      translation-consensus position estimator (Sections~\ref{sec:kdtree}--\ref{sec:vote}).
\item \textbf{A rigorous discriminability study on UAV-VisLoc.}
      Using a truth-anchored retrieval diagnostic, we show that per-element
      geometric matching hits a \emph{discriminability ceiling} at city scale
      (retrieval $0\%$ in the top-$20$ despite $93\%$ coverage), that it is
      robust to a battery of descriptor enrichments (size, constellation
      context, building-level granularity, normalization---each shrinking the
      true-match distance up to $16\times$ without effect), and that a
      CFBVM-style particle filter cannot recover it because the true position is
      supported in only $12\%$ of frames. The ceiling is terrain-general: it
      holds across five UAV-VisLoc sites spanning urban, coastal, hilly,
      built-up, and arid terrain (Section~\ref{sec:persite}). We conclude that
      the limiting factor is the observation model, and identify what a
      scale-robust one must supply (Sections~\ref{sec:analysis}--\ref{sec:seq}).
\item \textbf{An area-level Ekeland descriptor that recovers discriminability.}
      Acting on the diagnosis, we aggregate the descriptor over ${\approx}200$\,m
      cells as a histogram of per-building Ekeland free-cone angles (with density
      and layout). This escapes the per-element ceiling and, against real
      re-implemented BRM and CFBVM baselines under identical protocol,
      significantly improves pooled coarse-localization recall across five
      sites ($n{=}272$, $p{\le}0.02$)---though the effect is not universal per
      site (best on 3 of 5 terrains)---isolating the Ekeland angle as a
      complementary, discriminative shape cue at the area level, with absolute
      accuracy remaining coarse (Section~\ref{sec:area}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and assumptions.}
The method assumes (A1) the UAV camera points approximately nadir (tilt
$<15^\circ$, corrected by IMU), (A2) heading $\phi$ is available from onboard
compass/IMU to within $\pm5^\circ$ (standard for consumer autopilots), and
(A3) buildings are present in the field of view.
The descriptor and matching stages require \emph{no training data specific to
the target scene}; only the Mask R-CNN backbone is pre-trained on public aerial
building datasets~\cite{ji2018building,fang2021building}.
We analyze the pipeline's behavior---including the role of building coverage
and the discriminability of the geometric descriptor---experimentally
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

\paragraph{Broader impact.}
A lightweight, training-free geo-localizer could benefit emergency response,
infrastructure inspection, and autonomous navigation in GPS-denied or
communication-restricted environments.
We acknowledge that the same capability carries dual-use implications in
contested or surveillance scenarios; we encourage responsible deployment with
appropriate access controls.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Classical template and feature matching.}
Template matching methods (NCC, Phase Correlation, Mutual Information) compare
query images to raster satellite tiles by direct pixel similarity.
NCC-based template matching is reported to require hundreds of seconds per
query and collapses under seasonal illumination shifts~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm}.
SIFT~\cite{lowe2004sift}, SURF~\cite{bay2006surf}, and
ORB~\cite{rublee2011orb} extract keypoints robust to scale and rotation, yet
the nadir-to-satellite appearance gap overwhelms these features: SIFT achieves
62.8\,m RMSE with zero Recall@10 on MAFS-10, while ORB achieves 21.1\,m with
only 7.9\% Recall@10~\cite{yuan2025swapf}.
The abBRIEF descriptor embedded in Monte Carlo Localization reduces error to
${\approx}17.8$\,m but is constrained to ${\sim}1$\,km$^2$
coverage~\cite{mantelli2019abbrief}.


\noindent\textbf{Polygon, shape, and visibility descriptors.}
Classical polygon descriptors offer a natural alternative for VMAP-based
localization.
Turning functions~\cite{arkin1991efsd} represent polygon shape as cumulative
turning angle along the boundary---invariant to scale and rotation but
sensitive to segmentation noise and vertex ordering.
Shape contexts~\cite{belongie2002shape} encode the distribution of boundary
points in a log-polar histogram around each vertex, offering partial
deformation invariance but requiring dense correspondences and scaling poorly
to large databases.
Polygon angle histograms aggregate interior angle distributions per building
instance, yielding a compact per-building signature but losing the spatial
inter-triangle context that discriminates geometrically similar buildings in
different surroundings.

Our MFCA is conceptually related to the \emph{visibility polygon}
and \emph{largest empty sector} problems from computational
geometry~\cite{devadoss2011discrete,orourke1987computational}: given a
query point inside a polygonal environment, the visibility polygon enumerates
all directions not obstructed by boundary segments.
MFCA is a coarser scalar summary---the exterior angle at each vertex of the
kernel expansion polygon $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$---but encodes building-shape
context through the expansion process, making it discriminative across
different polygon geometries, not just visible directions.
Crucially, our construction is motivated by Ekeland's variational
principle~\cite{ekeland1974} rather than being an ad hoc design choice:
the stopping criterion of the kernel expansion (original triangle vertices
must remain on the boundary) is geometrically analogous to the Ekeland
criticality condition, and the edge-anchored angle realises the ``cone
touches but does not cross'' picture directly, via a single closed-form
expression (Definition~\ref{def:ekeland_angle}).
The result is an $O(1)$-per-vertex exact computation, with no search radius
and no angular sweep, that was previously approximated by a
$1^\circ$-sweep.
This grounding distinguishes MFCA from visibility-polygon summaries, which
are defined by geometric coverage alone rather than by a variational
stability criterion.

Our descriptor operates on \emph{triangles within a building}, not
whole-polygon statistics, and encodes building-shape context through the
edge-anchored Ekeland angle---a structural quantity unavailable to any
single-polygon descriptor.


\noindent\textbf{Semantic vector map matching.}
Vector map (VMAP) methods avoid raster storage and cross-modal appearance gaps.
Choi \& Myung~\cite{choi2020brm} proposed Building Ratio Map (BRM), matching
building pixel-density ratios per grid cell against OpenStreetMap; it achieves
7.5--12\,m RMSE in a 5.7\,km$^2$ area but discards shape information
and degrades at large scale.
Nassar et al.~\cite{nassar2018deep} applied deep CNN segmentation and
shape-based matching, reporting 6.3--10.4\,m average drift across two flight
datasets (Potsdam, Famagusta).
Ouyang et al.~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm} proposed Coarse-to-Fine Building Vector
Matching (CFBVM) combined with a GMM particle filter, reporting 6.6\,m mean
error over 11,025\,km$^2$ using a shape vector defined over concentric
density rings (as reported in the published abstract/summary; we could not
access the full text to verify experimental detail independently).
Most closely related to our positive result, Wang et al.~\cite{ssrm2024}
match buildings jointly on \emph{shape and spatial relationship} (SSRM) against
clean vector (`.shp') e-maps, explicitly motivated by the observation---which
our analysis independently quantifies---that per-building \emph{shape matching
alone cannot capture building uniqueness}. A key distinction is the map source:
SSRM matches against precise vector footprints, whereas we match UAV-segmented
footprints against \emph{satellite-segmented} ones, where cross-view
segmentation disagreement (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}) makes exact per-building
correspondence infeasible---which is why our discriminability gain appears at the
area level rather than per building.
Our method shares the VMAP paradigm with CFBVM but substitutes ring-area
summation with a triangle-level angular descriptor that requires no overlap
area computation, is invariant to segmentation polygon ordering, and carries
explicit free-space context through the kernel-expansion MFCA; unlike CFBVM's
shape-agnostic density rings, its area-level form retains per-building shape via
Ekeland-angle histograms (Section~\ref{sec:area}).


\noindent\textbf{Deep learning and sequential methods.}
End-to-end cross-view networks
(FPI~\cite{dai2022fpi}, SSPT~\cite{fan2024sspt},
Goforth \& Lucey~\cite{goforth2019gps}) learn appearance-invariant embeddings
but require scene-specific retraining to generalize.
The Semantic-Weighted Adaptive Particle Filter (SWA-PF)~\cite{yuan2025swapf}
combines SegFormer~\cite{xie2021segformer} semantics with 4-DoF particle
filtering, achieving state-of-the-art 6.57\,m RMSE on MAFS-10 in 7\,s as a
sequential method, but trains on the target terrain.
Kinnari et al.~\cite{kinnari2022season} address season-invariant UAV
localization directly, reporting 16.6--30.6\,m mean error depending on
condition; their follow-up LSVL~\cite{kinnari2023lsvl} scales this to
100\,km$^2$ via large-scale season-invariant retrieval, reporting
12.6--18.7\,m mean error---both require CNN pretraining on seasonal image
pairs and operate in sequential mode.
A 2025 hierarchical matching approach~\cite{hieruav2025} combines DINOv2
semantic features with structural constraints and RANSAC homography refinement,
reporting 17.7--18.7\,m mean error on the AerialVL and CS-UAV benchmarks;
it requires DINOv2 pretraining and is not training-free.
Sequential methods~\cite{conte2009vision,shan2015google} fuse visual
measurements with odometry; integrating our descriptor into such a filter
is a natural future extension.


\noindent\textbf{Recent geometry-aware and foundation-model methods.}
\begin{sloppypar}
Recent work on cross-view geo-localization has moved toward 3-D geometry
grounding and large-scale multimodal models.
(MGS)$^2$-Net~\cite{mgs2_2026} and drone-video-to-BEV approaches such as
Video2BEV~\cite{video2bev2024} obtain strong cross-view performance by
explicitly modeling 3-D geometry and aligning views in bird's-eye-view
space; these represent a higher performance bar than classical BRM/CFBVM.
LVLM-based re-rankers (e.g., SkyLink~\cite{skylink2026}) demonstrate
effective candidate scoring improvements when paired with retrievers;
while heavier than our approach, they represent a complementary retrieval
stage in a potential two-stage pipeline.
Training-free, geometry-first methods such as ours offer an appealing
low-footprint, explainable alternative for rapidly deployed platforms
where model retraining is impractical.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $I_{\mathrm{UAV}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ be a nadir UAV
image with flight altitude $h$ (m) and compass heading $\phi$ (degrees from
North).
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a geo-referenced satellite map with ground sampling
distance $\delta_{\mathrm{sat}}$ (m/px) and known WGS84 origin.

We extract $M$ triangle descriptors
$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{UAV}} = \{f_i\}_{i=1}^{M}$ from $I_{\mathrm{UAV}}$
and pre-extract $N$ satellite descriptors
$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sat}} = \{(f_j, p_j, \ell_j)\}_{j=1}^{N}$ from
$\mathcal{M}$, where $p_j \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is the pixel centroid of triangle
$j$ in the satellite map and $\ell_j$ is its parent patch identifier.
The localization problem is to recover the UAV's 2-D geographic position
$(x^*, y^*)$ by identifying the satellite patch whose building triangles best
match those in $I_{\mathrm{UAV}}$.
We use the $\ell_1$ (cityblock) distance $\|a - b\|_1 = \sum_{k=1}^{5}|a_k - b_k|$
throughout. For each UAV descriptor $f_i$, the $K$ nearest satellite
neighbors under $\|\cdot\|_1$ are retrieved:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_i = \bigl\{j^* :
  \|f_i - f_{j^*}\|_1 \le d_{(K)}\bigl(\{f_j\}_{j=1}^N, f_i\bigr)\bigr\},
\quad i = 1,\ldots,M,
\label{eq:retrieval}
\end{equation}
where $d_{(K)}(\cdot, f_i)$ denotes the $K$-th smallest $\ell_1$ distance
to $f_i$ over the satellite descriptor set.
All matched satellite triangles vote for their parent patch; the predicted
patch $\ell^*$ is the plurality winner:
\begin{equation}
\ell^* = \underset{\ell}{\arg\max}
\;\bigl|\{(i,j) : j \in \mathcal{C}_i,\;\ell_j = \ell\}\bigr|.
\label{eq:vote}
\end{equation}
The predicted UAV position $(x^*, y^*)$ is the centroid of the building
triangles of patch $\ell^*$ (Section~\ref{sec:vote}).

\noindent\textit{Illustrative example.}
Suppose $M=3$ UAV descriptors $\{f_1,f_2,f_3\}$ are matched against $N=6$
satellite descriptors with $K=2$.
After retrieval, the matched pairs and their parent patches are:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
UAV $f_i$ & Sat.\ $f_j$ & $\|f_i-f_j\|_1$ & Patch $\ell_j$\\
\midrule
$f_1$ & $f_2^s$ & 4.1° & $\ell_A$ \\
$f_1$ & $f_5^s$ & 5.8° & $\ell_A$ \\
$f_2$ & $f_1^s$ & 3.2° & $\ell_A$ \\
$f_2$ & $f_4^s$ & 6.7° & $\ell_B$ \\
$f_3$ & $f_3^s$ & 2.9° & $\ell_A$ \\
$f_3$ & $f_6^s$ & 8.1° & $\ell_C$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Patch $\ell_A$ receives 4 votes, $\ell_B$ and $\ell_C$ each receive
1 vote; hence $\ell^* = \ell_A$ and $(x^*,y^*)$ is its geographic centroid.

This formulation is restricted to \emph{2-D position} (latitude, longitude).
Step~1b (Section~\ref{sec:preproc}) performs \emph{yaw alignment} using the onboard compass heading $\phi$, correcting image orientation prior to matching. This is distinct from \emph{full rotation estimation} from geometric matching alone, which remains future work.

\paragraph{Map modality.}
Our method processes raster satellite GeoTIFF patches from UAV-VisLoc through
the same Mask R-CNN segmentation pipeline used for UAV images.
Building polygons are extracted from raster tiles; no external OSM vector
layers are required.
The offline descriptor database occupies approximately 12\,MB per site
(5-D float32 entries, ${\approx}600\text{k}$ triangles/site).

\paragraph{Query protocol.}
Because the method requires no scene-specific training, no train/test split is
needed: \emph{all} UAV images of each site are used as queries, and the full
satellite map of that site is used as the reference.
Queries from which no building polygon can be segmented produce no descriptor
and are counted separately as uncovered queries (coverage is reported alongside
the error metrics).
The hyperparameters below (Table~\ref{tab:impl}) are fixed by design from
prior literature and geometric reasoning about the sensor and map resolution,
not tuned on any held-out split of UAV-VisLoc.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our pipeline has two phases, mirroring the design of successful vector-map
localizers. \emph{Phase~1} (Steps 1--6, Sections~\ref{sec:preproc}--\ref{sec:refine})
turns a single image into an ambiguous \emph{observation}---a set of candidate
positions from purely geometric building-footprint matching. \emph{Phase~2}
(Section~\ref{sec:phase2}) integrates these per-frame observations over a
trajectory with a Gaussian-mixture/particle-filter estimator, exactly as
CFBVM~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm} and SWA-PF~\cite{yuan2025swapf} do. We describe
Phase~1 first, as our descriptor is its core contribution.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%

\subsection{Query Pre-processing}
\label{sec:preproc}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Raw UAV images contain yaw offset, roll/pitch tilt, and altitude-dependent
scale relative to the satellite GSD.
Normalizing these geometric distortions before segmentation improves polygon
fidelity without requiring any learned component.

\paragraph{Design.}
Given altitude $h$, heading $\phi$, roll $\rho$, and pitch $\psi$ from the
per-image CSV metadata:

\noindent\textbf{Step 1a --- Scale normalization.}
The UAV image GSD is $\delta_{\mathrm{UAV}} = h \cdot p_s / f_l$, where $p_s$
is the sensor pixel pitch and $f_l$ the focal length (from EXIF).
The image is isotropically rescaled by
$s = \delta_{\mathrm{UAV}} / \delta_{\mathrm{sat}}$.

\noindent\textbf{Step 1b --- Yaw alignment.}
The image is rotated by $-\phi$ so that north aligns with the image top.

\noindent\textbf{Step 1c --- Roll/pitch correction.}
Small tilt offsets are removed by an affine shear
$A = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & \tan\rho \\ \tan\psi & 1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$.
For UAV-VisLoc nadir flights $|\rho|,|\psi|<3^\circ$, displacement is
negligible ($<1\%$); the step is included for generality.

\noindent\textbf{Step 1d --- Central crop and resize.}
The normalized image is center-cropped and resized to $500\!\times\!500$\,px.

\paragraph{Technical advantage.}
Heading alignment (assumed $\pm5^\circ$ accuracy) reduces rotational ambiguity
in subsequent matching without any learned model.

\paragraph{Implementation note.}
The UAV-VisLoc per-image metadata provides no focal length $f_l$ or pixel
pitch $p_s$, so Step 1a is realised as an altitude-ratio rescale
$s = h_{\mathrm{ref}} / h$ to a reference altitude $h_{\mathrm{ref}} = 400$\,m
rather than the exact GSD ratio; since the downstream descriptor uses only
angular quantities, this affects segmentation scale consistency but not
descriptor validity.
The residual scale mismatch is removed by an empirical calibration of the crop
factor so that building sizes match between the UAV and satellite branches
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis} uses this calibrated setting).

\subsection{Building Segmentation (Step 2)}
\label{sec:seg}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Extracting building polygons from the raster image is the only learned
component in the pipeline; all downstream steps are purely geometric.

\paragraph{Design.}
Images are processed by Mask R-CNN~\cite{he2017maskrcnn} with a ResNet-50-FPN
backbone, pre-trained on aerial building
datasets~\cite{ji2018building,fang2021building} at confidence threshold 0.5.
Each connected mask region is converted to a polygon by: (i)~finding connected
components, (ii)~extracting the outer contour, and (iii)~applying the
Douglas-Peucker algorithm~\cite{douglas1973peucker} at tolerance
$\tau=2$\,px, yielding a set of building polygons
$\mathcal{P} = \{P_1,\ldots,P_K\}$.

\paragraph{Technical advantage.}
Because the downstream descriptor depends only on triangle \emph{angles}, not
polygon size, colour, or texture, the method tolerates common segmentation
errors---merged adjacent buildings, partial occlusions, and small road
false-positives~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm}.

\subsection{Constrained Delaunay Triangulation (Step 3)}
\label{sec:cdt}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
A polygon with $V$ vertices has no natural fixed-cardinality encoding, making
whole-polygon descriptors difficult to compare across buildings with differing
vertex counts.
Triangulation addresses this while delivering two additional properties.
First, \emph{matching density}: a building with $V$ vertices yields $V-2$
triangle descriptors via CDT, each an independent matching hypothesis.
A single building thus contributes up to $V-2$ votes to the patch estimator,
dramatically increasing robustness against individual false positives relative
to whole-polygon methods that produce a single vote per building.
Second, \emph{graceful degradation}: if segmentation merges two buildings into
one polygon, CDT still produces valid triangles from the merged shape, many of
which correctly match their satellite counterparts---a robustness property
that whole-polygon methods lack, since a merged polygon no longer resembles
either original building individually.

\paragraph{Design.}
For each polygon $P_k$ with ordered vertices $v_1,\ldots,v_{V_k}$,
Constrained Delaunay Triangulation (CDT) is applied.
The constrained edge set
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{constrained}}^{(k)}
= \bigl\{(v_i,\,v_{i+1 \bmod V_k})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{V_k}
\label{eq:cdt_edges}
\end{equation}
consists of all polygon boundary edges, which must appear in every CDT
of $P_k$ and prevent any interior edge from crossing the building boundary.
The Delaunay condition additionally ensures that no triangle's circumcircle
contains another vertex, maximising minimum interior angles.
This produces a mesh $T^{(k)} = \{t_1,\ldots,t_{|V_k|-2}\}$ of
non-overlapping triangles (by the Euler relation for simple polygons).

\paragraph{Technical advantage.}
CDT maximizes the minimum interior angle over all triangulations of the same
vertex set~\cite{de1997computational}, avoiding sliver triangles whose
near-zero angles produce near-degenerate descriptors.

\subsection{MFCA Feature Extraction (Step 4)}
\label{sec:feat}
\label{sec:mfca}

\paragraph{Interior angle components.}
For triangle $t$ with vertices $v_A,v_B,v_C$, the three interior angles
$\beta_A,\beta_B,\beta_C$ (reserving $\alpha$ for the descriptor and
kernel-expansion quantities below) are computed analytically via the law of
cosines.
The two smallest are selected and sorted:
$\alpha_1 \le \alpha_2$, both in $(0^\circ, 180^\circ)$.
These two components encode the \emph{shape} of the triangle independent of
position, orientation, or scale.

\paragraph{Ekeland's variational principle and its geometric analogy.}
\label{para:ekeland}

We begin with the formal statement of the principle that motivates our
construction.

\begin{definition}[Ekeland's Variational Principle~\cite{ekeland1974}]
\label{def:ekeland}
Let $(X,d)$ be a complete metric space and
$f: X \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}$ be lower-semicontinuous and bounded
below.
For every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every $u \in X$ with
$f(u) \le \inf_X f + \varepsilon$, there exists $v \in X$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $f(v) \le f(u)$ \hfill\emph{(improvement)}
  \item $d(v,u) \le 1$ \hfill\emph{(proximity)}
  \item $f(v) < f(x) + \varepsilon \cdot d(v,x)$ \;for all $x \ne v$
        \hfill\emph{(strict cone criticality)}
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Geometrically, condition (iii) says that no point $x \ne v$ can improve
$f$ beyond what the distance $d(v,x)$ ``pays for''.
Equivalently, the graph of $f$ is touched from below by an upward cone
of slope $\varepsilon$ at $v$, and this cone cannot be raised without
losing contact.
This \emph{cone-touching} interpretation is the direct inspiration for the
Ekeland angle.

\begin{remark}[Geometric analogy, not a formal instantiation]
\label{rem:ekeland_analogy}
The construction is \emph{motivated} by Ekeland's variational principle
(EVP), not a literal instantiation of it: the polygon boundary
$\partial P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ plays the role of the metric space, the negated
free half-angle at a vertex plays the role of the bounded-below function, and
the kernel-expansion stopping condition (no further merge is possible without
pushing an original vertex into the interior) mirrors EVP's critical-point
condition that no improvement is available without a proportional distance
cost. We do not invoke EVP's analytic machinery (completeness,
lower semicontinuity, existence in non-compact or infinite-dimensional
spaces)---that apparatus guarantees a critical point \emph{exists}, which is
not in question here, since $e_X$ is a finite, explicitly computable
geometric angle. The value of the analogy is constructive: it is what
motivated extracting an exterior angle capped at $180^\circ$ as the
descriptor's free-space component, not a claim of mathematical equivalence.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Basic properties.}
Two facts follow immediately from Definition~\ref{def:ekeland_angle} and are
stated here without dedicated theorem environments, since both are direct
consequences of the closed form
$e_X = \min(360^\circ - \gamma_{\mathrm{int}}(X), 180^\circ)$ rather than
results requiring proof: (i)~\emph{monotonicity}---merging more triangles
into $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ can only increase or maintain the interior angle
$\gamma_{\mathrm{int}}(X)$, so $e_X$ is non-increasing under kernel
expansion, meaning vertices in complex building recesses (large
$P_{\mathrm{sub}}$, reflex angles) receive a strictly smaller $e_X$ than
isolated convex corners ($e_X = 180^\circ$); and (ii)~\emph{coordinatewise
separation}---two descriptor vectors differ whenever any one of their five
coordinates differs, by exactly that coordinate's difference under the
$\ell_1$ norm. Property (ii) is a statement about vector arithmetic, not
about whether the descriptor is discriminative among real building
populations: Section~\ref{sec:analysis} shows empirically that it is not, at
the scale of a city map, and quantifies exactly why (impostor density at the
true-match distance, not coordinate separation, is the binding constraint).


\paragraph{Motivation.}
Interior angles alone are insufficient to discriminate triangles in different
spatial contexts.
Consider a right-angle building corner in two locations: one at an isolated
building (all adjacent CDT triangles merge readily into $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$,
keeping the vertex convex), the other at a concave building recess (merging
adjacent triangles creates a reflex configuration at the original vertex).
Both corners may share identical interior-angle sub-vectors
$(\alpha_1 = 45^\circ, \alpha_2 = 45^\circ)$.
The Ekeland angle discriminates them: the convex vertex (exterior angle
$\alpha \ge 180^\circ$) yields $e_v = 180^\circ$ (exterior free space is wide),
while the reflex vertex (exterior angle $\alpha < 180^\circ$) yields
$e_v = \min\!\bigl(\angle(\hat{\mathbf{a}}', X\mathbf{d}),\,
\angle(\hat{\mathbf{b}}', X\mathbf{d})\bigr) < 180^\circ$ via the
axis-parallel reflection (exterior free space is compressed).
The discrimination is encoded directly in the shape of $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$:
the maximal expansion polygon whose stopping criterion (all three original
vertices stay on the boundary) is the Ekeland criticality condition.

\paragraph{Obstacle set.}
The obstacle for the Ekeland angle computation at each vertex $v$ is
\emph{solely the boundary of $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{O}_v \;=\; \partial P_{\mathrm{sub}}
  \;=\; \{\text{boundary edges of the kernel expansion polygon } P_{\mathrm{sub}}\}.
  \label{eq:obstacle}
\end{equation}
This is geometrically motivated: $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is the maximal polygon
whose stopping condition mirrors Ekeland's criticality---its boundary is
therefore the tightest obstacle that the principle's cone can touch without
crossing.
No external neighbor polygon set or search radius $r$ is required.

\paragraph{Exact edge-anchored definition.}
Let $X \in \{v_A,v_B,v_C\}$ be an original triangle vertex on $\partial P_{\mathrm{sub}}$,
and let $\mathbf{a}$ (from $X$ toward $X_{\mathrm{prev}}$) and
$\mathbf{b}$ (from $X$ toward $X_{\mathrm{next}}$) be the two $\partial P_{\mathrm{sub}}$
boundary edges incident to $X$.
Let $\gamma_{\mathrm{int}}(X)$ denote the interior angle of $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$
at $X$, measured inside the polygon, and let
$\gamma(X) = 360^\circ - \gamma_{\mathrm{int}}(X)$ denote the corresponding
exterior (free-space) angle at $X$
($\gamma(X) = \angle\mathbf{a}X\mathbf{b}$ measured through the exterior of
$P_{\mathrm{sub}}$).

\begin{definition}[Ekeland Angle at vertex $X$]
\label{def:ekeland_angle}
\begin{equation}
  e_X \;=\; \min\bigl(\gamma(X),\,180^\circ\bigr).
  \label{eq:ekeland_angle}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Axis-parallel reading, and why heading error cannot destabilize $e_X$]
\label{rem:axis_reading}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ekeland_angle} admits an equivalent geometric construction
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:mfca}, Phase~2; Fig.~\ref{fig:feature_extraction}): draw
any ray $X\mathbf{d}$ parallel to the $O_x$ or $O_y$ axis inside the exterior
wedge $\angle\mathbf{a}X\mathbf{b}$, and reflect the boundary edge directions
$\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ across it; the reflected ray that
stays outside $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is tangent to the opposite boundary edge, at
angle $\gamma(X)$ to $X\mathbf{d}$---the geometric picture behind Ekeland's
``cone touches but does not cross'' condition.
Because $\gamma(X)$ is a fixed property of $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ at $X$ and does
not depend on \emph{which} of the four axis-parallel rays is drawn, $e_X$
itself never depends on an axis choice: there is no discrete decision
boundary at which $e_X$ can jump as the coordinate frame rotates.
Compass-heading error therefore propagates
into the pipeline only through the Step~1b yaw-alignment image rotation
(which changes which triangles Mask~R-CNN segments and how CDT triangulates
them), never through a discontinuity internal to $e_X$'s own definition.
This construction is \textbf{exact} (no angular quantization),
\textbf{$O(1)$ per vertex} (one interior-angle computation and a $\min$),
and \textbf{deterministic} (no supremum-not-attained edge case).
\end{remark}

\noindent\textit{Empirical check.} We test this claim directly rather than
resting on the analytical argument alone: re-querying two sites (urban,
hilly; $100$ frames each) with synthetic heading error injected at $2^\circ$,
$5^\circ$, and $10^\circ$ before yaw alignment changes median end-to-end
error and meter-level accuracy by only a few percentage points, with no
consistent degradation as heading error grows (e.g.\ site~01 MA@500m:
$2.0\%$ at $0^\circ$ vs.\ $3.1$--$4.2\%$ at $2^\circ$--$10^\circ$). This is
consistent with the remark's claim: error here is dominated by the
discriminability ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:analysis}, not by heading
misalignment, so injecting further heading noise has little additional
effect to measure.


\paragraph{Algorithm.}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Axis-Parallel Reflection Ekeland Angle at triangle vertices}
\label{alg:mfca}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Triangle $T=(v_A,v_B,v_C) \in P_k$, CDT mesh $\mathcal{T}^{(k)}$,
         max depth $D_{\max}$
\Ensure $(e_{v_A},\,e_{v_B},\,e_{v_C})$

\smallskip
\State \textbf{--- Phase 1: Ekeland Kernel Expansion (build $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$) ---}
\State $P_{\mathrm{sub}} \leftarrow T$;\quad $\mathrm{depth} \leftarrow 0$
\Repeat
  \State $\mathrm{merged} \leftarrow \mathtt{false}$
  \For{each $t'$ sharing an edge with $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$, $t' \in \mathcal{T}^{(k)}$}
    \State $P' \leftarrow P_{\mathrm{sub}} \cup t'$ \quad\Comment{tentative merge}
    \If{$v_A,\,v_B,\,v_C$ all remain on $\partial P'$ (none becomes interior)}
      \State $P_{\mathrm{sub}} \leftarrow P'$;\quad
             $\mathrm{merged} \leftarrow \mathtt{true}$;\quad \textbf{break}
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State $\mathrm{depth} \mathrel{+}= 1$
\Until{$\lnot\,\mathrm{merged}$ \textbf{or} $\mathrm{depth} = D_{\max}$}
\Comment{Invariant: $v_A,v_B,v_C \in \partial P_{\mathrm{sub}}$}

\smallskip
\State \textbf{--- Phase 2: Ekeland Angle (Definition~\ref{def:ekeland_angle}) ---}
\For{each $X \in \{v_A,v_B,v_C\}$}
  \State Compute $\gamma_{\mathrm{int}}(X) \leftarrow$ interior angle of $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ at $X$
  \State $\gamma(X) \leftarrow 360^\circ - \gamma_{\mathrm{int}}(X)$
  \State $e_X \leftarrow \min\bigl(\gamma(X),\,180^\circ\bigr)$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $(e_{v_A},\,e_{v_B},\,e_{v_C})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Correctness of Phase 1 (termination and invariant).}
Each iteration merges at most one triangle; $|\mathcal{T}^{(k)}|$ is finite;
therefore Phase 1 halts in at most $\min(|\mathcal{T}^{(k)}|, D_{\max})$
iterations.
The invariant $v_A,v_B,v_C \in \partial P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is maintained
by construction: a merge is accepted only if all three original vertices
stay on the boundary of the merged polygon.
The stopping condition is the direct Ekeland criticality condition: when
no merge can be accepted, $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is the \emph{maximal expansion}
of $T$ consistent with the criticality constraint---exactly as Ekeland's
principle guarantees a point $v$ beyond which no improvement is possible
without paying a proportional distance cost.

\noindent\textbf{Correctness of Phase 2.}
Phase 2 directly evaluates the closed form of
Definition~\ref{def:ekeland_angle} (Remark~\ref{rem:axis_reading} gives the
equivalent axis-parallel-reflection reading): one interior-angle computation
and one $\min$ per vertex, hence exact and $O(1)$ per vertex with no
axis-dependent branching and no supremum-not-attained edge case.

\noindent\textbf{Local sensitivity vs.\ population-level discriminability.}
The Ekeland angle is locally sensitive to building geometry: a triangle in a
concave building recess expands into a $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ that creates one or
more reflex vertices at the original triangle vertices, yielding
$e_X < 180^\circ$, while a triangle at an isolated convex corner stays
convex throughout expansion, yielding $e_X = 180^\circ$ (monotone in the
expansion depth, Remark~\ref{rem:ekeland_analogy}). This means two
same-shape triangles in geometrically different local contexts always
receive different descriptor vectors---but that pairwise fact says nothing
about whether the descriptor is discriminative among the $10^5$--$10^6$
candidates present on a real city map, since many \emph{different} building
recesses can produce similar $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ geometry and hence similar
$e_X$. Section~\ref{sec:analysis} measures this directly: at the scale of a
real satellite map, the density of near-identical impostors around the
true match's descriptor value dominates, not the coordinatewise separation
established above. The kernel-expansion depth $D_{\max}$ controls how much
surrounding context is incorporated ($D_{\max}=0$ removes the expansion
entirely, reducing $e$ to the constant convex value $180^\circ$); we
ablate $D_{\max}$ empirically in Section~\ref{sec:dmax} rather than
fixing it by an untested design argument.

\paragraph{5-D descriptor assembly.}
The MFCA triplet is sorted in ascending order to yield $(e_1 \le e_2 \le e_3)$,
independently of the interior-angle sort.
The complete descriptor is:
\begin{equation}
f \;=\;
\bigl(\underbrace{\alpha_1,\;\alpha_2}_{\text{shape}},\;
      \underbrace{e_1,\;e_2,\;e_3}_{\text{free-space}}\bigr)
\;\in\;(0^\circ,\,180^\circ)^5.
\label{eq:descriptor}
\end{equation}
The two groups are sorted \emph{independently} to preserve their distinct
geometric roles: mixing them would corrupt the scale-invariance of the shape
sub-vector, since cone angles and interior angles occupy different angular
regimes.
Figure~\ref{fig:feature_extraction} illustrates the Ekeland angle geometry
and the complete extraction pipeline.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_descriptor}

    (all computed by the actual kernel-expansion code, not hand-derived).
    \textbf{(a)} A convex vertex: the free cone (green) saturates at
    $e_v = 180^\circ$ at every expansion depth $D_{\max}$, since no merge ever
    introduces a reflex angle there---this is why a plain interior angle alone

    \textbf{(b)} A shallow, obtuse recess (grey CDT mesh; seed triangle

    \textbf{(c)} A sharp, acute recess (same construction, $3$ merges): the

    $e_v = 36.8^\circ < 90^\circ$. Together the three cases span the

    quickly a given recess surfaces also depends on where CDT happens to
    place the seed triangle relative to it, which is why $D_{\max}$ is swept
    empirically rather than fixed by assumption (Section~\ref{sec:dmax}).
    Three such scalars, one per triangle vertex, form the free-space sub-vector
    $(e_1, e_2, e_3)$ of the 5-D descriptor
    $f = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2, e_1, e_2, e_3)$.}
  \label{fig:feature_extraction}

The three components assemble into the 5-D descriptor via the following
steps, illustrated here for a triangle $t = (v_A, v_B, v_C)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.},leftmargin=*,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Interior angles.}
  Compute $\beta_A, \beta_B, \beta_C$ analytically; sort the two smallest
  to obtain $\alpha_1 \le \alpha_2 \in (0^\circ, 180^\circ)$.
\item \textbf{Ekeland (free-cone) angles.}
  For each vertex $v \in \{v_A, v_B, v_C\}$, run
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:mfca} to obtain $e_v \in (0^\circ, 180^\circ)$;
  sort the triplet to yield $e_1 \le e_2 \le e_3$.
\item \textbf{Concatenate.}
  $f = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2, e_1, e_2, e_3) \in (0^\circ, 180^\circ)^5$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textit{Example.}
For a right-isosceles triangle ($\beta_A = 90^\circ$, $\beta_B = \beta_C =
45^\circ$) sited at an open corner with per-vertex free cones
$e_{v_A} = 106^\circ$, $e_{v_B} = 88^\circ$, $e_{v_C} = 72^\circ$,
the descriptor is $f = (45^\circ,\, 45^\circ,\, 72^\circ,\, 88^\circ,\,
106^\circ)$.
The shape sub-vector $(45^\circ, 45^\circ)$ is shared with any right-isosceles
triangle, but the free-space sub-vector $(72^\circ, 88^\circ, 106^\circ)$
is specific to the footprint geometry surrounding the triangle, making $f$
discriminative at the scene level.


\subsection{KD-Tree Offline Indexing and Online Matching (Step 5)}
\label{sec:kdtree}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Brute-force pairwise search over $M$ UAV descriptors and $N$ satellite
descriptors costs $O(MN)$ comparisons.
For a single UAV query ($M \approx 300$) against a typical site database
($N \approx 600$k), this is $1.8 \times 10^8$ $\ell_1$ evaluations per image---
prohibitive for real-time operation.

\paragraph{Offline indexing.}
Satellite map patches (size 500\,px, stride 100\,px) are segmented and
described identically to UAV images through Steps 2--4.
Descriptors, centroids, and patch labels are stored in
$\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{sat}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 5}$, indexed by a
KD-tree built with \texttt{scipy.spatial.KDTree} (leaf size 40, $\ell_1$ metric).

\paragraph{Online query.}
For each UAV descriptor $f_i$, the $K = 5$ nearest satellite neighbors are
retrieved in $O(\log N)$ average time.
Total online query cost is $O(M\log N)$.
In practice, $M=300$ queries against $N=600$k take ${\approx}0.8$\,s on a
single CPU core.

\paragraph{Technical advantage.}
$\ell_1$ distance is more robust than $\ell_2$ to outlier angle components because it
does not square residuals; this matters for sorted-angle vectors where a
single corrupted component would disproportionately inflate $\ell_2$ distances.

\subsection{Plurality-Vote Position Estimator (Step 5b)}
\label{sec:vote}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
A single descriptor match is unreliable: even a geometrically discriminative
descriptor has non-zero false-match probability at the scale of
$N \approx 600\text{k}$ satellite triangles.
Plurality voting aggregates $M \times K$ match-votes from all triangles in the
query image.
The key statistical property \emph{would be} that false matches scatter
\emph{uniformly} across satellite patches (a spurious descriptor match
equally likely to land anywhere in the map), while true matches
\emph{concentrate} on the single correct patch---converting per-triangle
uncertainty into a patch-level signal that grows more reliable as $M$
increases, with the vote tally's signal-to-noise ratio scaling as $\sqrt{M}$
under a uniform false-match model. We state this design assumption here and
flag immediately that Sections~\ref{sec:impostor}--\ref{sec:analysis}
measure it directly and find it does \emph{not} hold at descriptor-collision
scale: impostors cluster around the true match's descriptor value rather
than scattering uniformly, so the $\sqrt{M}$ argument does not rescue
retrieval in practice, however clean the design intent.
Let $\ell_j$ be the patch identifier of satellite triangle $j$.
Each pair $(i,j)$ with $j \in \mathcal{C}_i$ casts one vote for patch
$\ell_j$, and the plurality winner is selected as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:vote}.
The predicted UAV position $(x^*,y^*)$ is the mean pixel centroid of the
building triangles indexed under patch $\ell^*$ (a building-weighted patch
centroid, which is slightly more precise than the geometric patch centre when
buildings are off-centre), converted from pixel coordinates via:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{lat} = p_y^* \cdot \delta_{\mathrm{sat}} / 111{,}320,\quad
\Delta\mathrm{lon} = p_x^* \cdot \delta_{\mathrm{sat}} /
  (111{,}320\cos\mathrm{lat}_0),
\label{eq:geoconvert}
\end{equation}
implemented equivalently as linear interpolation within the map's
georeferenced corner bounds.

\paragraph{Discussion.}
Plurality voting is robust to individual false matches: a segmentation error
that generates a spurious descriptor will vote for a random patch, whereas
the correct patch accumulates coherent votes from many triangles belonging
to the same building cluster.
The patch centroid itself is resolution-bounded by the patch stride
(100\,px $\times$ 0.3\,m/px = 30\,m); Step~6 below removes this bound.

\subsection{Sub-Patch Refinement (Step 6)}
\label{sec:refine}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Reporting the winning patch's own centroid caps resolution at the patch
stride, so even a perfect vote cannot achieve sub-30\,m error on its own.

\paragraph{Design.}
Step~1 already normalizes heading and scale before segmentation, so any
residual misalignment between a UAV triangle centroid and its matched
satellite triangle centroid is well modeled as a pure 2-D translation.
Let $\mathcal{W}$ be the set of matched pairs $(i,j)$ with $j \in \mathcal{C}_i$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:retrieval}) whose satellite triangle $j$ belongs to the
winning patch $\ell^*$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:vote}); each pair implies a translation
$t_{ij} = p_j - c_i$, where $c_i$ is the pixel centroid of UAV triangle $i$
in the preprocessed frame and $p_j$ is satellite triangle $j$'s pre-computed
centroid.
A one-point RANSAC selects the translation hypothesis with the largest
agreeing subset (inliers within 15\,px, $\approx$4.5\,m at the map's GSD---a
fixed, reasoned default, not tuned on held-out data) and averages that
inlier set to obtain $\hat{t}$.
The refined position is $\hat{t}$ applied to the UAV image's own nadir point
(its center in the preprocessed $500{\times}500$ frame, per assumption A1).
When fewer than 3 correspondences land in the winning patch, refinement is
skipped and the coarse patch centroid is reported unchanged---refinement
never degrades the estimate.

\paragraph{Technical advantage.}
Because $\hat{t}$ is estimated from a robust inlier consensus rather than a
single correspondence, refinement tolerates individual false matches within the
winning region. We note, however, that refinement only sharpens an already
\emph{correct} coarse match; our analysis (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}) shows the
coarse match itself is rarely correct at full-map scale, so this stage does not
by itself change the localization outcome---it is included for completeness and
for use once a coarse prior makes the winning region reliable.

\subsection{Phase 2: Sequential Integration}
\label{sec:phase2}

Steps 1--6 constitute \emph{Phase 1}: given one image they produce a set of
candidate positions, i.e.\ an \emph{observation}. Because a single frame's
observation is ambiguous (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}), the accurate vector-map
methods do not act on it directly---they integrate observations over a
trajectory. CFBVM~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm} describes each frame's matches as a
Gaussian mixture and fuses them in a particle filter; SWA-PF~\cite{yuan2025swapf}
runs a 4-DoF particle filter over semantic matches. We adopt the same
\emph{Phase 2} design so that our descriptor can be evaluated as the matching
stage of a complete pipeline.

\noindent\textbf{Observation model.}
For frame $t$, the Phase-1 matches vote for a UAV nadir position
$N = p_j - (c_i - c_0)$ per matched pair $(i,j)$, where $c_0$ is the image
centre; the resulting vote cloud is summarized as a Gaussian mixture
$z_t(\mathbf{x}) \propto \sum_{m} \exp(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mu_m\|^2/2\sigma^2)$ over
its modes.

\noindent\textbf{Particle filter.}
A set of $P$ particles $\{\mathbf{x}^{(p)}_t\}$ is propagated by a random-walk
motion model (isotropic Gaussian of standard deviation matched to the measured
inter-frame displacement) and weighted by the observation likelihood
$z_t(\mathbf{x}^{(p)}_t)$; systematic resampling with a small global-reseed
fraction recovers from divergence, and a credibility test down-weights frames
whose vote cloud is diffuse (analogous to CFBVM's credibility indicator). The
weighted mean is the per-frame position estimate.
Section~\ref{sec:seq} evaluates this full two-phase pipeline; we find that
Phase~2 cannot rescue localization here, and we trace the cause precisely to
the Phase-1 observation model.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

We evaluate on the UAV-VisLoc benchmark, focusing the detailed analysis on the
Changjiang-20 site (site~01; a ${\approx}9{,}774\times26{,}762$\,px map yielding
$N\!\approx\!4.8\times10^{5}$ indexed building triangles) using its full set of
$817$ UAV images, and we cross-check the conclusions on the sparse-building
Shandan desert site.
Rather than reporting only a single position error---which, as we show below, is
governed by matching failure rather than by the estimator---we instrument the
pipeline to isolate \emph{where} it breaks, and report:
(i)~\textbf{Coverage} (\%): the fraction of query locations whose true position
has at least one segmented satellite building nearby, which upper-bounds
achievable recall;
(ii)~\textbf{Retrieval} (\%): a truth-anchored diagnostic---for each UAV triangle
whose correct satellite counterpart is fixed by ground truth, the fraction for
which that geometrically correct match appears among the top-$K$ nearest
descriptors. This measures descriptor \emph{discriminability} directly,
independent of any voting or refinement stage;
(iii)~\textbf{Position error} (m), with recall at $30/50/100$\,m: the ground
distance between the true position and the prediction obtained by aggregating
the per-triangle matches with a translation-consensus (Hough) vote;
(iv)~\textbf{Sequential error} (m): the error of a trajectory-level estimate that
integrates the per-frame matches with a Gaussian-mixture observation and a
particle filter (Section~\ref{sec:seq}), following the coarse-to-fine,
particle-filter paradigm of CFBVM~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm} and
SWA-PF~\cite{yuan2025swapf}.
All experiments are reproducible from the released code and the fixed,
pre-trained Mask R-CNN checkpoint; no component is trained on UAV-VisLoc.

\subsection{Coverage is not the bottleneck}
\label{sec:coverage}

We first rule out the obvious failure mode. For $92.9\%$ of queries, the true
UAV location has at least one segmented satellite building within $150$\,m
(Table~\ref{tab:analysis}, first row): the correct place is present in the map
and is segmented. Any localization failure is therefore a \emph{matching}
failure, not a coverage or segmentation-recall failure.

\subsection{The discriminability ceiling}
\label{sec:analysis}

Table~\ref{tab:analysis} reports the truth-anchored retrieval and position
error under a sequence of increasingly informative descriptors, all on the same
segmentation output of the Changjiang-20 site ($N\!\approx\!4.8\times10^{5}$
building triangles, $98$ queries with ground truth).
The finding is stark and consistent: \emph{the geometrically correct match is
retrieved in $0\%$ of the top-$20$}, and the position error stays at the
kilometre scale, regardless of how the descriptor is enriched.
Adding building size, a log-polar building-constellation context (a
Belongie-style shape context~\cite{belongie2002shape} computed over neighbouring
building centroids), matching at the building rather than triangle level, and
per-dimension standardization each \emph{reduce the true-match descriptor
distance}---by up to $16\times$ (from $\ell_1\!=\!38$ to $2.4$ under
standardization)---yet none lifts retrieval off $0\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:disc}).

This is the key diagnosis: the failure is not that the correct match is far in
descriptor space, but that the descriptor is too \emph{low-entropy} to be
\emph{unique}. At $10^{5}$--$10^{6}$ candidate elements, thousands of
geometrically near-identical footprints sit as close to the query as the true
one does; shrinking the true-match distance shrinks the impostors' distances
equally. Enriching per-element features cannot escape this, because the
information that makes a \emph{location} unique is not carried by any single
element---it is the spatial arrangement of many, which per-element retrieval
does not exploit.
Only when the search is constrained to a small area (a coarse prior) does the
error fall---to $146$\,m at ${\approx}0.2$\,km$^{2}$---but this merely reflects
the window mechanically bounding the error rather than genuine identification:
sub-$30$\,m recall is $2.1\%$, barely above the ${\approx}1.4\%$ expected from a
uniform guess inside the window, and the error grows monotonically with the
search area, from $146$\,m at $0.2$\,km$^{2}$ to $2.2$\,km at $50$\,km$^{2}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:scale}). The descriptor contributes almost no localization
signal beyond what the prior supplies.

\subsection{Impostor density: what actually bounds retrieval}
\label{sec:impostor}

Section~\ref{sec:mfca}'s basic properties establish that the descriptor is
coordinatewise separating (two vectors differ whenever their generating
geometry differs) --- a fact about vector arithmetic, not about
discriminability among real building populations. Here we measure the
quantity that does govern discriminability: the density of impostors (other
map elements) around the true match's descriptor value, at the scale of a
real city map. For a query triangle and its geometrically correct satellite
match at $\ell_1$ distance $d_{\mathrm{true}}$, define the impostor count as
$N \cdot P(\text{random map element within } d_{\mathrm{true}})$, where $N$
is the map's total element count; this directly estimates the true match's
expected rank, and hence whether it can appear in a top-$K$ list at all.

Table~\ref{tab:impostor} reports this for all four analysed sites, together
with the effective dimensionality of the 5-D descriptor via the PCA
participation ratio $(\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2$ (a scale-free
measure of how many components actually carry variance). Two findings
follow. First, the descriptor's effective dimensionality is
$2.0$--$2.3$, confirming quantitatively that most of its variance lives in
$2$--$3$ of the $5$ nominal dimensions (consistent with $e_2,e_3$ staying
near the constant convex value $180^\circ$ for the majority of vertices).
Second, and decisively, at the true-match distance between
$15\%$ and $34\%$ of the \emph{entire} candidate population sits closer than
the true match itself (site-dependent: $14.9\%$ on the hilly site to $33.5\%$
on the coastal site), giving an expected true-match rank in the tens to
hundreds of thousands---orders of magnitude beyond any practical $K$. This
is the real, measured account of why retrieval collapses to $0\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:analysis}): not because the true match is far in descriptor
space, but because so many impostors are equally close.

The per-dimension standardization ablation (Table~\ref{tab:analysis}) makes
this concrete. Standardizing shrinks the true-match $\ell_1$ distance by
$16\times$ on the urban site ($38\!\to\!2.4$); measured independently here
across all four sites, standardization shrinks the raw true-match distance by
$14$--$18\times$ (site-dependent), yet Table~\ref{tab:impostor}'s z-scored
column shows the expected rank stays essentially \emph{unchanged} on every
site ($103$k vs.\ $113$k on the urban site; comparable on the other three)---
because standardization rescales impostor distances by the same factor.
Shrinking the true-match distance without shrinking the surrounding impostor
density does not improve retrieval; this is the quantitative form of the
discriminability ceiling.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the kernel-expansion depth $D_{\max}$}
\label{sec:dmax}

$D_{\max}$ is fixed to $4$ throughout the main results; we ablate it here
rather than leave it an untested design choice, sweeping
$D_{\max}\in\{0,1,2,4,8\}$ on both the per-element retrieval diagnostic
(sites 01--03) and the area-level recall (all five sites). $D_{\max}=0$ is
the natural control: it disables kernel expansion entirely, so every $e_X$
collapses to the constant convex value $180^\circ$ and the Ekeland
component of the descriptor carries no information beyond a single fixed
constant (the two interior-angle dimensions $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are
unaffected by $D_{\max}$ and remain informative at every depth).

Table~\ref{tab:dmax} reports both halves. Per-element retrieval stays at
$0\%$ from $D_{\max}=0$ through $D_{\max}=4$, and is effectively still $0\%$
at $D_{\max}=8$ ($0.03\%$ pooled across the three sites---a single triangle
on the coastal site, not a trend). This holds even though the true-match
$\ell_1$ distance itself \emph{grows} substantially with $D_{\max}$ on the
urban site (median $17\to18\to22\to38\to51$ at
$D_{\max}=0,1,2,4,8$) as more context is folded into each descriptor: a
larger, more spread-out true-match distance has no effect on retrieval,
reinforcing Section~\ref{sec:impostor}'s account that distance to the true
match is not the binding constraint at any depth---impostor density is.
Area-level recall tells a different, and more informative, story: pooled
recall rises from $4.0\%$ at $D_{\max}=0$ (already above the plain
baseline's depth-independent $2.9\%$, because the interior-angle histogram
alone adds signal) through $4.4\%$ at $D_{\max}\in\{1,2\}$ to a peak of
$5.9\%$ at $D_{\max}=4$, then falls back to $5.1\%$ at $D_{\max}=8$.
$D_{\max}=4$ is thus not an arbitrary choice: it is close to the
empirically-measured optimum, with both less context ($D_{\max}\le2$) and
more ($D_{\max}=8$) giving measurably worse pooled recall.

\subsection{Sequential integration does not recover it}
\label{sec:seq}

Since every accurate map-matching method integrates ambiguous per-frame
observations over a trajectory (CFBVM's Gaussian-mixture matches in a particle
filter~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm}; SWA-PF's 4-DoF particle
filter~\cite{yuan2025swapf}), we test whether the same estimator rescues our
descriptor. On a $100$-frame consecutive flight segment we run a particle filter
($2\times10^{4}$ particles, random-walk motion of $\approx\!266$\,px/frame
matching the measured inter-frame displacement) whose per-frame observation is
the Gaussian-mixture of our descriptor's Hough votes.
It does not converge (Table~\ref{tab:seq}, Figure~\ref{fig:seq}): the
trajectory-level error stays at kilometre scale (filtered median $5.5$\,km,
\emph{worse} than the single-frame median of $3.2$\,km, because the filter
commits to a spuriously consistent wrong track) and sub-$100$\,m recall is $0\%$.
The diagnostic explains why: the true position receives \emph{any} vote in only
$12\%$ of frames (median $0$ votes/frame; Figure~\ref{fig:seq}a), and when
present its region ranks $\approx\!123$rd. A particle filter integrates a signal
that \emph{recurs}; ours is absent from $88\%$ of frames, so the filter locks
onto a spurious track (Figure~\ref{fig:seq}b). The limiting factor is thus the
\emph{observation model}: unlike CFBVM's area-density descriptor, our
per-element geometric descriptor does not provide recurring, spatially
consistent support for the true location, which no downstream filter can
manufacture. Figure~\ref{fig:votecloud} makes this concrete on one frame---the
descriptor's votes scatter across the entire map rather than concentrating at
the true position.

\paragraph{Runtime and scale.}
The pipeline is nonetheless efficient and compact, as intended:
descriptor extraction and a single $K$-NN query take ${\approx}0.8$\,s per image
on one CPU core, and the site-01 index of $4.8\times10^{5}$ elements occupies
${\approx}12$\,MB ($5\times$float32 per element). These costs scale linearly in
the number of indexed elements; the barrier to city-scale use is accuracy, as
analyzed above, not compute or memory.

\subsection{Discussion of the failure}

The analysis above identifies a single dominant failure mode and rules out
several plausible alternatives. It is \emph{not} primarily a coverage problem
(the true location is mapped in $93\%$ of cases), nor a segmentation-density
problem specific to sparse terrain (the ceiling appears on dense-urban
Changjiang), nor a voting or refinement problem (the correct match is absent
from the top-$20$ before any voting), nor an estimator problem (a particle
filter cannot converge on it), nor a retrieval-scheme problem (constellation
hashing with geometric consensus verification, Section~\ref{sec:quadhash},
fails for the same underlying reason). It is a \emph{discriminability} problem intrinsic
to per-element geometric matching: individual building triangles---even enriched
with size and neighbourhood context---are not unique among $10^{5}$--$10^{6}$
candidates, and the discriminative information that identifies a location lives
in the joint spatial configuration of many buildings, which per-element
retrieval discards. The one regime in which the descriptor localizes is when an
external coarse prior shrinks the candidate set (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}),
which is precisely how successful vector-map methods such as
CFBVM~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm} deploy their matching stage.

\subsection{A third line of evidence: constellation hashing also fails, and why}
\label{sec:quadhash}

Before pivoting to area-level aggregation, we tested whether a stronger,
training-free per-element matcher could recover discriminability directly:
astrometry.net-style geometric hashing~\cite{lang2010astrometry} over building
centroids. For every building we form a 4-point ``quad'' with its three
nearest neighbours, compute a similarity-invariant 4-D hash (the two most
separated points fixed to $(0,0)$--$(1,1)$; we verified the hash's invariance
and the closed-form similarity-transform recovery on synthetic data, $200/200$
trials each), and index all quads in a $k$-d tree. At query time, each UAV
frame's quads are looked up against this index; each candidate match yields a
full similarity transform (translation, rotation, scale) via closed-form
least squares, verified by how many of the frame's \emph{other} buildings
project onto a nearby satellite building under that transform.

This fails too, and the failure is informative. We tested three
verification-gate configurations across two sites of contrasting density
(dense urban, sparse coastal): unconstrained scale and scale gated near the
calibrated value $0.96$--$1.0$ (both on the urban site), and scale and
rotation jointly gated near zero, consistent with yaw-aligned frames (on the
coastal site, as a cross-site check). Median error stays at
$1.9$--$3.9$\,km across all three---no better than single-element matching.
A direct diagnostic isolates why: even applying the
\emph{known, correct} transform (ground-truth position, calibrated scale,
zero rotation) and counting how many satellite buildings fall near each
projected UAV building, the count is barely distinguishable from applying a
\emph{random, wrong} translation at the same scale (mean $4.5$ vs.\ $3.3$
buildings within $25$\,px on the dense site; mean $3.2$ vs.\ $0.6$ on the
sparse site, where a coincidentally dense reference cluster inflates the null).
Two effects compound: cross-view segmentation disagreement means only a
minority of buildings visible in a UAV frame have a corresponding detection
in the satellite map even at the true location, and building density is high
enough everywhere that a wrong transform still lands near \emph{some}
buildings by chance. With hundreds of candidate quads verified per frame, the
best \emph{wrong} candidate's building-count regularly matches or exceeds the
true candidate's---geometric verification cannot discriminate a correct
correspondence from a lucky coincidence at this density, independent of how
the candidates were retrieved. This is a third, independent measurement of
the same discriminability ceiling: per-element retrieval, hard theoretical
bounds on impostor density (Section~\ref{sec:impostor}), and geometric
consensus verification all hit the same wall from different directions.

\subsection{Area-level aggregation recovers discriminability}
\label{sec:area}

The diagnosis above is also constructive: if the discriminative signal lives in
the joint configuration of many buildings rather than in any single element,
then the descriptor should be aggregated over an \emph{area} rather than matched
per element. We test this directly while keeping the Ekeland angle central.
We tile each map into $\approx200$\,m cells and describe every cell by a
translation-invariant descriptor: building density, layout moments
(centroid spread, mean nearest-neighbour spacing), a footprint-size histogram,
and---the shape signal a density field lacks---\emph{histograms of the
per-building Ekeland free-cone angles}. Each UAV frame is matched, as a single
cell, against the satellite cells; we report coarse-localization recall
(fraction of frames whose true cell is in the top-$50$ of ${\sim}10^{3}$
cells).

To ground this in a genuine head-to-head rather than our own reconstruction,
we compare four cell descriptors under \emph{identical} segmentation, cells,
and protocol: a \emph{plain} descriptor (density + layout + size, the
information a generic area-density field carries); our own re-implementation
of \textbf{BRM}~\cite{choi2020brm} (a sub-grid building-area coverage ratio);
our own re-implementation of \textbf{CFBVM}'s~\cite{ouyang2024cfbvm}
concentric-ring building-area profile; and \emph{plain augmented with
Ekeland-angle histograms} (ours). BRM and CFBVM here are genuine
re-implementations evaluated on our data, not the authors' own reported
numbers (which appear only as landscape context in Table~\ref{tab:main}).

\paragraph{Statistical treatment.} With $70$--$90$ scored frames per site,
a single-site recall delta of a few percentage points is not distinguishable
from noise, and we do not present it as such. For every comparison we report
a bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval (frame resampling, $10^4$ replicates)
and a paired permutation test, both per site and pooled across all five
sites ($n=272$ scored frames); an effect is called significant only when the
pooled CI excludes zero.

Table~\ref{tab:area} and Figure~\ref{fig:area} give the full picture, which
is genuinely mixed and we report it as such. Area-level matching
\emph{escapes the per-element ceiling} on every descriptor: recall rises from
the flat $0\%$ of single-element retrieval to several times chance
($\approx3.7\%$ at top-$50$) in most cases. Ekeland is the best performer on
three of five sites (urban, coastal, hilly); on the built-up site our own
\emph{plain} descriptor is best, and on the arid site \emph{both} real
baselines outperform Ekeland. Pooled across all five sites, Ekeland
significantly outperforms plain ($+2.9$\,pp, $95\%$ CI $[0.7,5.1]$,
$p{=}0.02$) and significantly outperforms the re-implemented BRM baseline
($+4.4$\,pp, $95\%$ CI $[1.5,7.7]$, $p{\approx}0.012$--$0.014$ by permutation
test and McNemar's test respectively); it is directionally, but not
significantly, ahead of the re-implemented CFBVM-rings baseline ($+2.6$\,pp,
$95\%$ CI $[{-}1.1,6.2]$, $p{=}0.23$). The pooled effect is significant at
top-$50$ but \emph{not} at top-$10$ ($+0.7$\,pp, CI $[{-}0.7,2.2]$,
$p{=}0.63$): the claim we make is specifically that Ekeland-angle histograms
add coarse-localization signal at top-$50$, not a blanket improvement at
every operating point. The free-cone angle supplies per-building shape
information that arrangement- or density-only descriptors (BRM, CFBVM's
regime) do not carry by construction---but the effect is modest, not
universal, and a real re-implemented CFBVM baseline is a genuinely close
competitor rather than one we clearly surpass.

We are deliberately precise about scope. These are \emph{coarse}-localization
gains: absolute recall remains single-digit-to-low-tens of percent, and the
top-$1$ cell is still usually wrong, so the method does not yet deliver precise
metric localization. The contribution is a measured, statistically qualified
demonstration that (i)~the area is the right matching unit for this problem
and (ii)~the Ekeland free-cone angle is a discriminative, complementary shape
cue at that level on most, not all, terrains. Closing the remaining gap to
precise localization plausibly requires a learned area embedding or a clean
vector map (as used by shape-and-relationship methods on `.shp'
e-maps~\cite{ssrm2024}); both are natural extensions of the Ekeland area
descriptor introduced here.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a scene-agnostic UAV visual geo-localization framework
whose core novelty is a purely geometric 5-D triangle descriptor combining
sorted interior angles and edge-anchored Ekeland Free-Cone Angles (MFCA).
The key design innovation is the \emph{kernel expansion} $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$:
the maximal polygon formed by merging CDT triangles while keeping all three
original triangle vertices on the boundary.
This stopping criterion is geometrically analogous to Ekeland's
cone-criticality condition, and the resulting Ekeland angle is computed
exactly in $O(1)$ per vertex via an interior-angle closed form,
replacing the earlier $1^\circ$-sweep approximation.
The descriptor is invariant to texture, colour, resolution, and season, and
requires no scene-specific training.
The design of the MFCA component is geometrically inspired by Ekeland's
variational principle~\cite{ekeland1974} (Remark~\ref{rem:ekeland_analogy}):
each element of the principle (complete metric space, bounded function,
cone-touching condition, critical point value) has a natural geometric
counterpart in our construction---an analogy that motivates the descriptor's
design and yields a closed-form computation with no sup-not-attained edge
case, rather than a literal instantiation of EVP's analytic machinery.
An offline KD-tree index reduces online matching to $O(M\log N)$; plurality
voting over satellite patches converts the matching output to a predicted 2-D
geographic position.

Our second contribution is an honest, quantitative characterization of this
descriptor as a UAV-localization matching stage. On UAV-VisLoc we find that,
although building coverage is high ($93\%$), single-frame per-element retrieval
never places the correct match in the top-$20$ and localization error stays at
the kilometre scale; that enriching the descriptor with size, a building-
constellation shape context, building-level granularity, and dimension
normalization shrinks the true-match distance up to $16\times$ without moving
retrieval off zero; and that integrating the per-frame matches over a trajectory
with a particle filter---the mechanism that makes CFBVM and SWA-PF
succeed---also fails to converge, because the true position receives recurring
observation support in only $12\%$ of frames.
The limiting factor is therefore the \emph{observation model}: purely geometric
per-element matching does not, on its own, provide the recurring, spatially
consistent support that scale-robust localization requires.
This is a useful negative result. It shows that the promising direction is not
a richer per-element descriptor but a fundamentally more discriminative
\emph{observation}---an area-level or configuration-level descriptor (as in
CFBVM's density rings) or a learned embedding---paired with the coarse-to-fine,
sequential estimation that the accurate methods already employ. Our released
code and analysis pipeline (\texttt{eval.py} and the diagnostic scripts) let
future work measure any candidate observation model against the same
retrieval, coverage, and sequential-support criteria.

Our third contribution acts on this diagnosis and, crucially, keeps the Ekeland
angle central (Section~\ref{sec:area}). Aggregating the descriptor over
${\approx}200$\,m cells---as a histogram of per-building Ekeland free-cone
angles alongside building density and layout---escapes the per-element
discriminability ceiling: coarse-localization recall rises from a flat $0\%$ to
several times chance. Against real re-implemented baselines (BRM, CFBVM's
concentric-ring descriptor) under identical protocol, Ekeland-angle histograms
significantly improve pooled coarse recall over plain density/layout
($+2.9$\,pp, $p{=}0.02$, $n{=}272$) and over BRM ($+4.4$\,pp, $p{\approx}0.012$),
and are directionally ahead of CFBVM-rings without reaching significance
($+2.6$\,pp, $p{=}0.23$). The picture is genuinely mixed per site---Ekeland
is best on three of five terrains, plain wins on the built-up site, and both
real baselines beat Ekeland on the arid site---which is what makes the pooled,
statistically qualified claim credible rather than a cherry-picked win. This
isolates the Ekeland free-cone angle as a discriminative shape cue at the
area level that arrangement- or density-only descriptors do not by
construction carry, on most but not all terrains, and points to the concrete
next step: a learned area embedding, or matching against clean vector
maps~\cite{ssrm2024}, built on the Ekeland area descriptor introduced here.

\paragraph{Limitations and scope.}
(i) Our multi-site evaluation covers five of the eleven UAV-VisLoc sites,
selected for terrain diversity (urban, coastal, hilly, built-up, arid); the
discriminability ceiling was consistent across all five, and the area-level
Ekeland gain is significant when pooled but is not the best performer on
every individual site (Section~\ref{sec:area}); the remaining six sites and
the full per-site error distributions are left to future work.
(ii) Our area-level result is a coarse-localization study (top-$K$ cell recall
among ${\sim}10^{3}$ cells); we do not yet demonstrate precise metric
localization, which we expect requires either a learned area embedding or a
clean vector map, and did not implement.
(iii) The method assumes approximately nadir views, buildings in the field of
view, and heading to $\pm5^\circ$; open terrain without buildings is out of
scope.
(iv) SWA-PF~\cite{yuan2025swapf} is, as of this writing, an arXiv preprint with
no peer-reviewed venue found; its numbers are reproduced as reported and should
be re-checked against a peer-reviewed version if one appears.
